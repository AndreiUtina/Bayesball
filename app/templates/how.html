How the ratings work

From a game's score to the leaderboard and the predictions

Bayesball doesn't describe a player with one number. For every player it keeps a belief: a best guess of their skill and how sure it is of that guess. After every game it updates the belief with Bayes' theorem, the rule for learning from evidence. This page explains each number on the site, step by step.

The examples below aren't written by hand: they are calculated live, by the same code that rates the real games, with the site's current settings.

1. A skill is a bell curve, not a number

Each player has two skills, {{ ex.att }} and {{ ex.dfn }}, and each one is a bell curve:

  • its centre, μ ("mu"), is the best guess;
  • its width, σ ("sigma"), is the uncertainty: about two times in three, the true skill lies within ±σ of the centre.

Skills are measured in goals, compared with an average player (0). Two players who are both +2 are expected to beat two average players by about 2 goals.

The overall skill is the average of {{ ex.att }} and {{ ex.dfn }}. The two are linked: a good attacker is probably at least a decent defender (the model starts with a correlation of {{ s.role_correlation }}). So a game played in {{ ex.att }} also teaches the model a little about your {{ ex.dfn }}.

4. What a game tells us

This is how the model describes a game:

goal difference (A − B) = strength of A − strength of B + luck

  • A 2v2 team's strength is the average of its attacker's {{ ex.att }} skill and its defender's {{ ex.dfn }} skill. In 1v1, each player's strength is their overall skill.
  • Luck is bell-shaped too, with a spread of β = {{ s.beta }} goals: even between two equal teams, the goal difference varies by about that much from game to game.
  • The winner always reaches {{ game_type.points_to_win or 10 }}, so the score says how much better a team played: a 10–2 win is stronger evidence than 10–8. Teams are neutral: there's no home side.

5. Bayes' theorem: learning from a result

P(skill | result) = P(result | skill) × P(skill)P(result)

In words: the new belief is the old belief, re-weighted by how well each possible skill explains the result.

  • P(skill), the prior: the belief before the game (sections 1 and 3).
  • P(result | skill), the likelihood: how probable this score would be if the skills had these values (section 4).
  • P(skill | result), the posterior: the belief after the game. It becomes the prior for the next game.
  • P(result) only scales the curve so its probabilities add up to 100%.

The old belief and the luck are both bell curves, and that makes the new belief a bell curve too, which can be calculated exactly in a few lines, with no simulation. It comes down to four steps:

  1. Predict the goal difference from the current beliefs.
  2. Surprise = the actual goal difference − the predicted one.
  3. Each skill used in the game moves by surprise × gain. The gain is high when the model is unsure about that skill, and low when the game was unpredictable anyway.
  4. The uncertainty shrinks: every game is new evidence.

What follows from this:

  • Surprising results move ratings more than expected ones.
  • New players (wide curves) move faster than established ones.
  • The margin counts, so winning by less than expected can lower a rating (section 7).
  • The role you played moves most; your other role follows a little, through the link.
Show the maths

Stack the skills of everyone in the game into one vector s with mean μ and covariance Σ (the two skills of each player are linked by ρ = {{ s.role_correlation }}). A vector h says how much each skill counts: +½ for Team A's attacker's {{ ex.att }} and defender's {{ ex.dfn }}, −½ for Team B's, 0 for skills not in play. In 1v1 it is ±½ on both skills of each player.

skills can driftΣ ← Σ + τ²I
predicted goal differencem = hᵀμ
its variance (skills + luck)v = hᵀΣh + β²
surpriser = observed − m
gain of each skillK = Σh / v
new best guessesμ ← μ + K·r
new uncertaintyΣ ← Σ − K Kᵀ v

This is the update of a Kalman filter, the exact Bayesian answer when everything is a bell curve. Afterwards each player keeps their own 2×2 part of Σ (see Limits).

The winners go down and the losers go up. The model doesn't reward a win as such, but how the result compares with what it expected. Over many games that is fairer: narrowly beating a weaker team isn't evidence of being much better than them.

8. Predictions

For any matchup, the model predicts the goal difference as a bell curve too:

  • its centre is the expected goal difference: strength of A − strength of B;
  • its width combines the luck (β) with the uncertainty of every player in the game.

P(A wins) = P(goal difference > 0) = Φ(m / √v)

That's the area of the curve to the right of zero. Here is the rematch from section 7, as predicted before it was played:

  • Likely range (90%): m ± 1.645 √v goals, capped at the race to {{ game_type.points_to_win or 10 }}.
  • Games with new players are predicted closer to 50%, because their wide curves add uncertainty.

9. Skills can change

Players improve, or get rusty. Before each game, the uncertainty of every skill in play grows a little (τ = {{ s.tau }} goals per game), so the ratings keep listening to new results and never freeze. Editing or deleting a past game recalculates every rating from the first game onwards, in date order.

10. Limits

  • The settings below are educated starting guesses. Once there are about 50 games, they'll be tuned to fit the real results.
  • A race to {{ game_type.points_to_win or 10 }} caps the goal difference; the bell-curve model treats it as open-ended. That's a good approximation for normal games, less so for very lopsided ones.
  • Each player's own two skills are linked, but links between players (for example "Ann and Bob have only ever played together") are dropped after each game. It's a standard shortcut that keeps the maths fast; in tests with 500 simulated games it recovered the true skills almost as well as the exact calculation.
  • A bad day, a new table or a sore wrist all look like luck to the model.
